LESSON 1

Clauses: the foundation of every punctuation question

Almost every punctuation question is secretly asking one thing: what is on each side of the blank? Learn these three pieces and the rest gets easy.

PieceWhat it isExample
Independent clauseSubject + verb + complete thought. Can stand alone as a sentence.Bees make honey.
Dependent clauseHas a subject and verb but starts with a word like although, because, when, if, since, while, which, who. Can't stand alone.Although bees make honey
PhraseNo subject–verb pair at all.after the storm · a salamander from Mexico
The cover testPut your finger over the blank. Read the left side alone, then the right side alone. Ask: "Could this be its own sentence?" Your answer decides the punctuation.
LESSON 2

Joining two complete sentences

When both sides are complete sentences, only five things can go between them.

MarkHow it worksExample
.Period. Always works.The storm ended. The sun came out.
;Semicolon. Works exactly like a period.The storm ended; the sun came out.
, andComma + FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). Needs both.The storm ended, and the sun came out.
:Colon. Only when the second sentence explains or proves the first.The game was canceled: the field had flooded.
—Single dash. Works like a colon.The game was canceled—the field had flooded.

Both sides complete

.;, + FANBOYS:—, alonenothing; and

A comma alone is a comma splice. Nothing at all is a run-on.

One side incomplete

,nothing;.

A semicolon or period here creates a fragment. Use a comma or no punctuation.

The Rosetta Stone was found in 1799, it helped scholars decode hieroglyphs.Comma splice: two complete sentences joined by a comma alone.
The Rosetta Stone was found in 1799; it helped scholars decode hieroglyphs.
Twin trickA period and a semicolon do the same job. If two answer choices are identical except one has a period and the other a semicolon, they can't both be right, so neither is right. Cross out both.
LESSON 3

Commas: four jobs, four never-dos

The four jobs of a comma

Bats sleep during the day, and they hunt at night.1. Before FANBOYS joining two complete sentences.
Although the Roman Empire split, its eastern half survived.2. After an introductory clause, phrase, or word (Although…, In 1440…, However…).
Marie Curie, who was born in Warsaw, won two Nobel Prizes.3. Around extra (nonessential) information, always as a pair.
Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large vacuole.4. Between items in a list of three or more. The PSAT uses the comma before "and."

Never put a comma…

The students who volunteered last weekend, sorted 400 cans.Between a subject and its verb, no matter how long the subject is.
The scientists discovered, a new species.Between a verb and its object.
She found evidence, that the theory was wrong.Before a "that" clause.
Mammals such as, whales and bats, nurse their young.Right after "such as" or "including."
Rule of thumbEvery comma needs a reason from the list above. If you can't name the job, the comma probably shouldn't be there. The answer with fewer commas is right more often than you'd expect.
LESSON 4

Extra information: open a pair, close the pair

If you can delete it and the sentence still works and keeps its main meaning, it's extra. Set it off with a matching pair.

Allowed pairs

, … ,— … —( … )

Never mix

, … —— … ,, … ;
The Silk Road—a network of trade routes, carried ideas as well as goods.Opened with a dash, closed with a comma.
The Silk Road—a network of trade routes—carried ideas as well as goods.

Titles and names

The biologist Rachel Carson wrote about pesticides.Title directly before the name: no commas. The name is essential (which biologist?).
Rachel Carson, a biologist, wrote about pesticides.Description after the name: commas. The description is extra.

Which vs. that

Which introduces extra information and takes commas. That introduces essential information and takes no commas.

The chloroplasts, which contain chlorophyll, capture sunlight.
The book that I borrowed is overdue.
LESSON 5

Semicolons, colons, and dashes

MarkBefore itAfter itExample
;Complete sentenceComplete sentenceTomatoes have seeds; botanists call them fruits.
:Complete sentenceA list, explanation, example, or quoteThe recipe needs three things: flour, eggs, and milk.
—Single dash: like a colon. Pair: like commas around extra info.Bees dance—it shows other bees where flowers are.
The recipe needs: flour, eggs, and milk."The recipe needs" is not a complete sentence, so no colon.
Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts; which contain chlorophyll."which contain chlorophyll" is not a complete sentence, so no semicolon.
Bonus semicolon jobIn a list where the items already contain commas, semicolons separate the items: "We visited Austin, Texas; Denver, Colorado; and Boise, Idaho." This shows up rarely, but it does show up.
LESSON 6

However, therefore, and their cousins

Words like however, therefore, moreover, consequently, thus, nevertheless, meanwhile, for example, and in fact are not FANBOYS. A comma alone can't join two sentences with them.

Many people think tomatoes are vegetables; however, botanists classify them as fruits.Between two complete sentences: semicolon (or period) before, comma after.
The results, however, were surprising.In the middle of one sentence: commas on both sides.
Many people think tomatoes are vegetables, however, botanists classify them as fruits.Comma splice. This is the most common "however" trap.
How to decideCover the "however." Are the parts before and after it complete sentences? Then it needs a semicolon or period in front of it.
LESSON 7

Subject–verb agreement

Find the real subject, then match the verb. Singular subject, singular verb (it runs). Plural subject, plural verb (they run).

  • Cross out the middle. Prepositional phrases (of the coins, in the lab) and extra info between commas never contain the subject.
  • Always singular: each, every, one, either, neither, everyone, nobody. Also group nouns like team, colony, collection, family.
  • "And" makes it plural: The cat and the dog are asleep.
  • Watch science plurals: mitochondria, bacteria, and phenomena are plural.
The collection of ancient Greek coins includes rare pieces.The subject is "collection," not "coins."
Each of the planets travels in an elliptical orbit.
One of the oldest maps appears on a clay tablet.
TrapThe PSAT puts a plural noun right before the verb to trick your ear. "The collection of coins include" sounds fine but is wrong.
LESSON 8

Pronouns and sound-alikes

  • Agreement: a singular noun takes it / its. A plural noun takes they / their. "The colony relies on its queen," because one colony.
  • Who is for people, which is for things.
OwnershipContractionPlace / other
its (the dog wagged its tail)it's = it is
their (their journey)they're = they arethere (over there)
whose (whose book?)who's = who is
youryou're = you are
Expand the apostropheRead "it's" as "it is" in the sentence. "The colony relies on it is queen" makes no sense, so you need "its."
LESSON 9

Verbs: form and tense

Every sentence needs a real main verb

These forms cannot be the main verb of a sentence on their own: -ing words (producing), to + verb (to produce), and having + verb (having produced).

The mitochondria, often called the powerhouse of the cell, producing energy.Fragment: there's no main verb.
The mitochondria, often called the powerhouse of the cell, produce energy.

Match the time

Look for time clues: dates (In 1440), words like currently, recently, next year, and the tense of the other verbs nearby.

In 1440, Gutenberg developed a printing press, which made books cheaper.
TrapA long subject with extra info in the middle makes you forget that the sentence still needs its verb. After cutting the middle, check: subject + verb?
LESSON 10

Possessives and plurals

FormMeaningExample
studentone studentA student asked a question.
studentsmore than one, nothing ownedThe students left.
student'sone student owns somethingThe student's essay won.
students'several students own somethingThe students' essays won.
children'sirregular plural + 'sThe children's books are upstairs.
Two questions1. Does it own the next word? If not, no apostrophe. 2. Is the owner one or many? One: 's. Many (ending in s): s'.
LESSON 11

Modifiers: who's doing the action?

An opening phrase describes whatever noun comes right after the comma.

Walking to school, the rain soaked my backpack.The rain wasn't walking to school.
Walking to school, I got my backpack soaked by the rain.
Having studied the fossil for months, it was concluded that it was a new species."It" didn't study the fossil.
Having studied the fossil for months, the researchers concluded that it was a new species.
How it looks on the PSATThe blank comes right after the opening phrase's comma. Ask "Who did that?" and pick the choice that starts with that person or thing.
LESSON 12

Transitions

Transition questions ask for "the most logical transition." Read the sentence before and the sentence after, decide the relationship yourself, and only then look at the choices.

RelationshipWordsAsk yourself
Addfurthermore, moreover, in addition, similarly, likewiseIs it more of the same idea?
Contrasthowever, nevertheless, by contrast, instead, stillDoes it go against what came before?
Cause → effecttherefore, as a result, consequently, thus, soIs it caused by what came before?
Examplefor example, for instance, specificallyIs it a specific case of the idea?
Sequencefirst, then, subsequently, finallyIs it the next step in time?
Restate / sum upin other words, that is, in short, ultimatelyIs it saying the same thing more clearly?
TrapWords in the same row mean nearly the same thing. If two choices are both "add" words, neither is likely right, because the test can't have two correct answers.
TEST DAY

The 10-step grammar checklist

  1. Compare the choices first. What changes between them (commas? verb? apostrophe?) tells you which rule is being tested.
  2. Read the whole sentence, not just the words around the blank.
  3. Cover the blank. Complete on both sides? Use . ; , + FANBOYS : or —. Not complete? Use a comma or nothing.
  4. Period and semicolon are twins. If both appear in otherwise identical choices, cross out both.
  5. Open a pair, close the pair. Match commas with commas and dashes with dashes.
  6. Never split a subject from its verb with a single comma.
  7. Cross out the middle to find the real subject, then match the verb.
  8. Expand apostrophes: it's = it is, they're = they are.
  9. Opening phrase? The noun right after the comma must be the one doing the action.
  10. When in doubt, fewer commas. Every comma needs a job.
